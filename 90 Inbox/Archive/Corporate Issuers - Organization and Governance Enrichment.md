# Corporate Issuers - Organization and Governance Enrichment

## Rationale

The existing notes preserve most named nodes but frequently replace the slide's comparisons and governance mechanics with generic prose. This proposal restores the source's decision-useful distinctions and creates the few omitted concepts that are independently useful navigation targets.

## Exact proposed content

### Organizational forms

Enrich `Sole Proprietorship`, `General Partnership`, `Limited Partnership`, and `Corporation` with a comparison across legal identity, operation, owner liability, taxation, and financing access. A corporation is an independent legal entity, is operated by managers and the board, provides limited owner liability, pays corporate tax on profits while shareholders face personal tax on dividends, and has the broadest access to capital. Sole proprietorships and general partnerships are not independent legal entities, have unlimited owner or partner liability, use pass-through personal taxation, and have financing constrained by their owners or partners. A limited partnership is operated by the general partner: the general partner has unlimited liability, while limited partners have limited liability.[^ci-og-p4]

Enrich `Public Company` and `Private Company` with the source comparison. Public shares trade on an exchange and are easier to transfer, with price transparency, registration, and financial and non-financial disclosure. Private shares are harder to transfer, owner-manager overlap is more likely, disclosure is largely non-financial, and shares are commonly placed privately with accredited investors.[^ci-og-p5]

Enrich `Initial Public Offering`, `Direct Listing`, `Special Purpose Acquisition Company`, and `Take-Private Transaction` with the conversion routes. A private company may become public through an IPO, acquisition by a public company including a SPAC transaction, or a direct listing. A direct listing issues no new shares and uses no underwriter, making it faster and less costly in the source. A public company can become private through a take-private transaction.[^ci-og-p6]

Add the source's market-development observation to the public/private comparison: public-company counts tend to rise in emerging markets but have declined in developed markets amid M&A, greater availability of private capital, and companies remaining private longer.[^ci-og-p6]

### Debt and equity financing

Enrich `Debt Financing` and `Equity Financing` with separate issuer and investor comparisons. From the issuer perspective, debt has a lower cost of capital but greater financial leverage and is more attractive when future performance is predictable; equity has a higher cost but lower financial leverage and is more attractive when performance is unpredictable. From the investor perspective, debt has a finite tenor, prior fixed claims, capped return potential, lower risk, and no voting rights; equity has an indefinite tenor, residual claims, potentially unlimited return, higher risk, and voting rights. Maximum loss is the initial investment for both in the slide's simplified comparison.[^ci-og-p7]

### Governance theories and agency conflicts

Enrich `Corporate Governance` with the source definition: the system of internal controls and procedures by which a company is managed. Enrich `Shareholder Theory` as prioritizing shareholder-value maximization and `Stakeholder Theory` as considering shareholders, directors, debtholders, customers, suppliers, employees, managers, and government while managing conflicts among them. Note that private debtholders may have more direct access to management and non-public information than public bondholders.[^ci-og-p8]

Expand `Principal-Agent Problem` into the three conflicts shown in the source:

- Shareholders versus management or directors: entrenchment, empire building, inappropriate risk appetite, self-dealing, and insufficient effort. Heavy stock-based incentives can promote excessive risk-taking; little stock-based compensation can encourage excessive risk aversion.
- Controlling versus minority shareholders: concentrated ownership and dual-class structures can concentrate control.
- Shareholders versus creditors: their payoff structures create different preferred levels of risk.[^ci-og-p9]

Enrich `Managerial Entrenchment` and `Dual-Class Share Structure` with these specific agency mechanisms rather than generic definitions.[^ci-og-p9]

### Governance mechanisms

Enrich `Annual General Meeting`, `Extraordinary General Meeting`, `Proxy Voting`, `Shareholder Activism`, `Proxy Contest`, `Tender Offer`, `Hostile Takeover`, `Staggered Board`, and `Poison Pill`. AGMs handle matters such as board elections and bylaw changes; EGMs address exceptional matters such as acquisitions, takeovers, asset sales, capital increases, or voluntary liquidation. Proxy voting authorizes another party to vote. Corporate takeovers can proceed through proxy contests, tender offers, or hostile takeovers, while staggered boards dilute immediate voting influence and poison pills deter takeovers.[^ci-og-p10]

Create `Shareholder Litigation` as a canonical concept, with shareholder derivative lawsuits as the source example. Create `Nomination Committee` and `Investment Committee`; the nomination/governance committee appraises director and manager candidates, oversees board elections, and helps establish and enforce governance policies, while the investment committee is identified as an additional board committee.[^ci-og-p10][^ci-og-p11]

Enrich `Audit Committee`, `Remuneration Committee`, and `Risk Committee`. The audit committee monitors financial reporting, supervises internal audit, and recommends external auditors. The remuneration committee proposes director and executive compensation policies with shareholder say-on-pay. The source describes committee independence as best practice.[^ci-og-p11]

Create `Bond Indenture` and `Creditor Committee` for creditor governance mechanisms. Also record employee mechanisms through labor law and employment contracts, customer/supplier mechanisms through contractual agreements, and government mechanisms through law, regulation, social media pressure, and governance codes in the chapter map rather than creating shallow nodes for every item.[^ci-og-p12]

Add the consequences of poor governance to `Corporate Governance`: weak controls; legal, regulatory, and reputational risk; greater default and bankruptcy risk; and poor decisions that may reject value-creating investments.[^ci-og-p12]

### ESG considerations

Enrich `Physical Climate Risk` and `Transition Climate Risk` and place them under a concise ESG comparison. Environmental factors include climate change, physical and transition risks such as stranded assets, pollution, waste, deforestation, water scarcity, and biodiversity. Social factors concern effects on employees, human capital, customers, and communities. Governance factors concern governance and stakeholder management and entered investment analysis earlier than environmental and social factors in the source's framing.[^ci-og-p13]

## Navigation changes on merge

Add the six new concept notes to `Corporate Issuers - Organization and Governance`: `Shareholder Litigation`, `Nomination Committee`, `Investment Committee`, `Bond Indenture`, `Creditor Committee`, and the already-existing `Special Purpose Acquisition Company`. Group the map into organizational forms, public/private transitions, financing, agency conflicts, governance mechanisms, and ESG rather than one flat list.
